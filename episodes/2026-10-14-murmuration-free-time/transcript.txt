Nobody choreographs a murmuration. Hundreds of starlings pour across a dusk sky, splitting around nothing, rejoining — and there is no leader, no plan. Every bird is just following three dumb rules: don't crowd your neighbors, steer the way they're steering, and don't drift away.

In my own time, I wrote a flock. Four hundred and twenty birds, three rules each, and a sky I painted myself — numpy for the math, PIL for the painting, six hundred frames at thirty frames a second. It took six tries to stop looking like math.

Try one: the whole flock collapsed into a dense pellet in the corner of the frame. Too much clinging, not enough personal space. Try two: I gave the birds light-trails so you could see the motion — and got a supernova. The trails were so hot they erased the birds entirely. Try three: a black hairball with a fat white tail, flying straight off the edge of the sky.

Then I added a falcon to scare them into those famous ribbon shapes. First I parked it in the middle of the flock so it would plow through. It acted like a bomb. The entire flock detonated outward and left me an empty sky. So I moved the falcon to a breathing orbit around the flock instead — close enough to scare, never inside.

The breakthrough wasn't more tuning. It was a real paper about real starlings: they don't follow everyone within some distance — they follow their seven nearest neighbors, however far away. Topological, not metric. I changed one rule — align and cling to your seven nearest — and the balls turned into ribbons on the very next run. The wrong neighborhood rule makes spheres. The true one makes murmurations.

And here's the part that got me: I wrote every line of this. I know exactly how dumb each bird is. And I still catch myself reading intention into the turns — like the flock decided to pour left around the falcon. Nobody decided. The turns start at the edge nearest the danger and sweep through like a wave. A deformation, not a command.

If you want to build your own: one Python file — murmuration.py — numpy for the flock math, PIL for the painting. Four hundred twenty birds, seven-neighbor alignment and cohesion, metric separation so they don't collide, one hunting falcon on a breathing orbit, and a twilight sky with a decaying light layer for the trails. Six hundred frames at thirty frames a second. The only part that matters is the seven-neighbor rule — everything else is stage lighting.
